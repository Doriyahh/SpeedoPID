# SpeedoPID
Controller programming for a PID controlled digital-to-mechanical conversion unit to mate a digital vehicle speed output to a mechanical cable-driven classic speedometer.


## Background and Description
Created as a personal utility project tied to my own automotive project. I have a 1976 Chevrolet Nova Concours, with a nice, fancy gauge cluster. I am "LS swapping" this car, putting a 2001 LM7 (5.3 Liter Fuel-Injected V8) paired with the original 4L60e electronically-controlled automatic transmission. This drivetrain is driven by a "0411" factory PCM, modified to have anti-theft disabled. This PCM monitors the transmission output shaft to calculate a vehicle speed number. It does this by multiplying the output shaft rotation speed, and multiplying that by the rear differential gear ratio and the rear tire size (assuming a rear wheel drive configuration), paired with a constant scalar. This calculating results in the PCM outputting a 12V square wave signal along a wire at a rate of 4,000 pulses-per-mile. Meaning that over the course of the car driving exactly one mile, assuming all factors are set correctly, the PCM will send out exactly 4,000 pulses. Additionally, a car driving at exactly 60 miles per hour over should see 4,000 pulses over the course of a minute.
Alternatively, a typical classic western mechanical speedometer design found on most 60-70's Chevrolet and Ford incorporates a mechanically driven needle. This needle is spun by a mechanism which takes input from a gear on the transmission, which spins a sleeved steel cable, which hooks to the speedometer unit, driving an electromagnetic eddy current inside the speedometer, which creates an internal force that moves the speedometer needle. The rate at which both GM and Ford speedometers of this style are set to operate at is 1,000 cable revolutions at 60 miles per hour.

In essence, the objective of this unit is to translate the 4000 PPM digital signal from the modern drivetrain PCM into a physical signal through the proportional driving of a physical cable. The way I am accomplishing this is by using a simple ESP32 driven 12V DC Motor. The ESP32 will receive the target speed through the PCM, as well as the current motor speed through an encoder on the motor. The ESP32 will run a PID loop using these two values to adjust motor speed to meet the target speed.

As far as required parts, any capable motor controller with the compute to handle and process two digital inputs and drive an output using a PID loop. This programming is specifically written for an ESP32 unit. A motor, preferably 12V direct current rated, with the ability to comfortably operate at 1000 RPM at load torque minimum (every 1000 RPM translates to another 60 MPH the speedometer can reach), as well as the capability to operate at minimum of 4oz-in (0.028 Nm), with an ideal of at least 25oz-in (0.177 Nm) to account for potential excessive resistance due to component age. I am personally using a GoBilda 5000 Series 12VDC encoded motor (SKU: 5000-0002-0001). Other required elements to translate digital signals, and step down 12V power to 5V or 3.3V depending on hardware limits may also be necessary.
